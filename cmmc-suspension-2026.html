⚠ September 2026 Regulatory Update

CMMC Phase 2 Suspended —
What You Still Owe the Government

Published September 2026  ·  ClearanceIQ Inc.  ·  6 min read

The DoD suspended third-party C3PAO assessments. That does not mean CMMC went away. Your SPRS score, your SSP, your POA&M, and your False Claims Act liability are all still fully active. Here is exactly what changed and what you still must do.

⚠ Do Not Confuse "Suspended" With "Cancelled"

The September 3, 2026 class deviation suspended CMMC Phase 2 third-party assessments. It did not suspend Phase 1 self-assessment requirements, SPRS submission, or False Claims Act liability. Every defense contractor with a DoD contract is still legally required to submit and maintain an accurate SPRS score.

What Actually Happened on September 3, 2026

The Department of Defense issued a class deviation on September 3, 2026 that suspended the requirement for CMMC Phase 2 third-party assessments by Certified Third-Party Assessor Organizations (C3PAOs). This was the formal certification process where an independent auditor would physically assess your cybersecurity posture before you could win certain DoD contracts.

That process — expensive, time-consuming, and complex — is on hold. But the confusion this created in the defense industrial base is dangerous. Many contractors read "CMMC suspended" and assumed their cybersecurity compliance obligations disappeared entirely. They did not.

The False Claims Act Risk Is Not Suspended

This is the part most contractors miss. The DOJ Civil Cyber-Fraud Initiative — launched in 2021 — allows the government to pursue False Claims Act cases against defense contractors who knowingly submit inaccurate SPRS scores. Since 2021, the DOJ has collected over $2.6 billion in settlements under this initiative.

The False Claims Act carries treble damages — three times the value of any affected contract — plus civil monetary penalties per false claim and potential federal criminal prosecution of individual officers who signed the attestation.

Legal Risk — 31 U.S.C. §§ 3729–3733

An Affirming Official who signs an inaccurate SPRS attestation is personally attesting to its accuracy. If that attestation is knowingly false or recklessly inaccurate, they face personal civil and criminal liability — not just corporate liability. The suspension of Phase 2 assessments does not change this exposure.

What You Are Still Required to Do Right Now

  • Conduct a self-assessment of all 110 NIST SP 800-171 Rev 2 security requirements
  • Calculate your SPRS score using the DoD Assessment Methodology (range: -203 to +110)
  • Submit your SPRS score to the Supplier Performance Risk System at sprs.army.mil
  • Maintain a current, written System Security Plan (SSP) — required by control 3.12.4
  • Maintain a Plan of Action & Milestones (POA&M) for any controls not fully implemented
  • Have an Affirming Official at your company sign an attestation confirming accuracy
  • Update your SPRS score within 30 days of any significant change to your security posture
  • Do NOT submit a score higher than your actual implementation — this is the core False Claims Act risk

Why So Many Contractors Are Confused

The terminology did not help. "CMMC" has become shorthand for the entire DoD cybersecurity compliance framework — but CMMC technically refers to the certification program that includes C3PAO assessments. The underlying requirements — DFARS 252.204-7012, NIST SP 800-171, and SPRS submission — exist independently of CMMC and were never suspended.

Think of it this way: suspending the driving test does not mean you can ignore traffic laws. The rules of the road are still fully in effect. You are still liable if you cause an accident. The only thing that changed is you no longer need to pass a formal test before getting in the car.

What This Means for Prime Contractors

If you are a prime contractor, you face an additional layer of responsibility. Under the DoD's flow-down requirements, you are responsible for ensuring your subcontractors also meet NIST SP 800-171 requirements. The suspension of C3PAO assessments does not eliminate your responsibility to verify your supply chain's cybersecurity posture before awarding subcontracts.

Primes who fail to adequately assess their subcontractors' cybersecurity compliance face potential contractual liability and False Claims Act exposure of their own.

✓ The Silver Lining

Self-certification is now the permanent enforcement mechanism — not a temporary workaround. This means you control your compliance timeline and cost. You do not need to pay $50,000–$138,000 for a C3PAO assessment. A properly documented self-assessment with an accurate SPRS score is fully compliant with current DoD requirements.

The Three Documents Every Contractor Needs

1. System Security Plan (SSP)

A written document describing your information systems, the boundary of those systems, and the implementation status of all 110 NIST SP 800-171 controls. Required by control 3.12.4 — this is what an auditor or contracting officer would ask for first.

2. Plan of Action & Milestones (POA&M)

For every control that is not fully implemented, your POA&M documents the gap, your planned remediation approach, and your target completion date. Controls worth 5 points must be addressed within 90 days; lower-weight controls within 180 days. This document proves you are actively working toward full compliance.

3. Affirming Official Attestation Letter

A signed legal attestation from a senior company officer confirming that your SPRS score is accurate and that the SSP and POA&M are current. This is the document that creates personal legal liability — the Affirming Official must understand what they are signing.
